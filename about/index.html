About Me

Software engineer at IBM · Electronic engineering background · Based in the UK

How I got here

I started out curious about electronics — the kind of curiosity that leads to taking things apart to see how they work, then trying to put them back together better than before. That led me to study electronic engineering. The degree covered digital logic, microprocessors, signal processing, and embedded systems, and it gave me a way of thinking about problems at a low level that has never really left me, even after moving entirely into software.

The unusual part: I completed that degree while working full-time. That wasn't the original plan — it was a practical necessity — but it turned out to be one of the more formative decisions of my career. Studying while doing a job teaches you to be ruthless about time, to focus on understanding rather than memorising, and to keep going when things are hard because the deadline doesn't move just because you're tired. Those habits stuck.

Once I had the degree, I joined IBM. I've been there ever since, which probably says something about the variety of work available when you're inside a company that builds software for some of the world's most demanding environments.

The IBM years

Most of my career at IBM has centred on CICS — IBM's transaction processing middleware that powers the core systems of banks, airlines, and government organisations worldwide. If you've ever used a cash machine, booked a flight on a legacy airline system, or filed a tax return, there's a reasonable chance CICS was involved somewhere in the chain.

Working on software at that scale changes how you think about quality. Defects aren't an inconvenience — they're an outage at a major bank. Regressions aren't embarrassing — they're front-page news. That context has shaped everything about how I approach engineering: the emphasis on testing, the preference for well-understood solutions over clever ones, the instinct to ask "what happens when this goes wrong?" before "how do I make this work?"

Over the years I've worked across the full software lifecycle — feature development, automated testing, build and CI/CD pipelines, developer tooling, and defect investigation. I've contributed to multiple major releases of CICS Transaction Server, built test automation frameworks that run thousands of scenarios against a live system, and dug into some genuinely interesting failure modes in complex distributed environments.

My current role sits at the intersection of software engineering and test engineering — writing production-quality code to test production-quality code. It's a discipline that demands you understand both the system under test and the mechanics of good testing, which I find more interesting than either in isolation.

How I approach work

  • Understand the problem firstBefore writing a line of code, I want to understand what's actually being asked and why. The right solution often isn't the obvious one.
  • Simple over cleverCode that's easy to read is easier to debug, maintain, and hand over. Complexity is a liability that compounds over time.
  • Test as you goTesting is part of development, not a phase that comes after. I write tests alongside the code — they're how I know the thing works.
  • Own it end to endI take responsibility from the first commit to production. If something I wrote breaks, I want to know about it and I want to fix it.
  • Communicate earlyA short conversation before starting saves hours of rework. Assumptions are expensive. I'd rather ask a dumb question than build the wrong thing.
  • Stay curiousTechnology moves fast and the interesting problems change. I stay current by building things, reading, and being willing to be wrong and learn from it.

Outside of work

I've been fascinated by aviation for most of my life — the engineering, the operational complexity, the sheer improbability of a 500-tonne aircraft crossing an ocean reliably enough to be a commodity. I follow airline news, aircraft development programmes, and the mechanics of how air traffic control actually works. It overlaps with an interest in travel, since one of the more compelling reasons to fly somewhere is the flying itself.

I'm also a football supporter — the kind who stays invested even when results don't justify it, which is most seasons. I follow the tactical side as much as the results: how teams set up, how managers adapt in-game, the interesting problems that modern football analytics is trying to solve.

Away from screens and sport, I read a lot of history — particularly the 20th century, where the pace of change was extraordinary and the human stories are vivid. And I watch films: science fiction mostly, the kind that uses a fictional setting to ask questions it would be harder to ask directly. The best of it ages well.

I also have a dog, which means two walks a day regardless of weather. It's turned out to be one of the more reliable habits I've built — genuinely good for clearing your head after a long session at the keyboard.
